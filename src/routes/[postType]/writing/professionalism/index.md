Everything that is mine is yours, and it has been that way
since the beginning of it all, before I had any choice in
the matter or an opportunity to pretend it could be anything
else. Everything that I want as a material desire for
myself, is yours, every impulse and luxury I attain and
possess only the property of the billions of us that have
made such experiences possible.

---

Under capitalism, there exists a basic and essential
contradiction between labor and capital: the needs of
capital are fundamentally opposed to the needs of those that
labor for it, and yet the two are bound inseparably
together. The immediate growth of capital lies in increasing
working hours, pay less in wages, preventing collective
bargaining; the needs of the laborers are precisely the
opposite. At the same time, they constitute each other, they
are what make each other exist; there would be no capital
were it not for the concept of laborers producing it and no
laborers if not the necessity of capital to interpellate
them as such. Capital's pursuit of its "own" interests
against those of labor thus has it undermine itself, as seen
in investments in automation and fixed capital leading to
the rate of profit to fall, the way depressed wages stunt
the economy as a whole and attack the market that capital
needs to sell its goods in, in the way undercutting
competitors to gain a profit lowers profits made system-wide
and long-term. In other words, capital and labor not only
have an antagonistic relationship but one that is constantly
unstable and dynamic, each side rearranging itself,
innovating and changing society in a direction we usually
recognize as "forward," as described by Marx's most famous
line (the history of all hitherto existing societies is the
history of class struggle).^[Though maybe this is one of
many instances where Marx's one-liners were more memorable
than instructive. This sentence has the pull that it does
because it conjures ideas of millions of workers with a
political program having moved history at every moment
(maybe an exagerration). I think Marx's actual usage of this
is much more diverse, like in his writing on automation as a
way to diminish the power of laborers, in his writing on the
working day, etc -- class struggle and the actors of history
cut both ways, and class struggles and their resolution
often take place less under an organized revolution than
under the anarchy of the market.]

The university is part of that society defined by and driven
by that contradiction, although it occupies a specific part.
The university has a neat air of being both within
capitalism and yet somewhat autonomous from both the state
and private industry, able to carry out functions neither
the apparatuses of the state or private industry saw as
central for their immediate gains but necessary nonetheless.
Basic science research, although ultimately needed by and
made possible by those intending to consume its downstream
products (say, a pharmaceutical product), may be more
usefully carried out by institutions that produce it as a
good in and of itself, that are not encumbered by specific
applications and have some autonomy with their research.
That relative autonomy, in other words, is exactly why the
university can be useful for capital and the state, both in
a material sense (division of labor) and in an ideological
sense (material relations are obfuscated when we buy into
the idea of academic workers as producers of knowledge for
the sake of itself). As Nick Mitchell writes in his 2019
essay
["Summertime Selves,"](https://thenewinquiry.com/summertime-selves-on-professionalization/)
which inspired this blog post, never are we more of it than
when we think we’re outside of it.

Thus is the perpetual struggle born: the university has its
interests that it serves which are fundamentally opposed to
the needs of the workers within it (one manifestation of the
labor-capital contradiction), but the appearance of those
interests can be deceiving. The university benefits from us
seeing ourselves as free from the interests of capital, free
to pursue our own interests, free to contest the university,
even; that's how it's able to use the outputs of our labor
to produce the culture, knowledge, patents, designs,
techniques it is expected to offer the state and private
industry.

I should note here that the university of course isn't
unique in this regard. Nonprofits, in all sorts of aid
programs, educational programs, cultural work, etc, pursue
ultimately ends servicing private industry and the state
(even if none of these parties will acknowledge them as
such). Even private industry and the state carry out each
others' interests in a structured unity while believing (in
part correctly) that they are doing so autonomously. In this
description, capitalism is like a vast machine that indeed
has an engine (a basic premise of capital accumulation, a
basic engine of the labor-capital contradiction), but also
millions of other components that all perform different
functions that act together and which cannot be discarded as
somehow less central or less important than that engine.
None of them can be properly seen as "noncapitalist" in any
absolute sense, and even if this machine is a complex unity
composed of other apparent modes of production, as a whole
it functions as a capitalist one. This is in no way to write
these individual components off with a blanket maneuever as
evil or to express a general pessimism around change, only
that I believe the next world will have to be born out of
this one.

The university as an institution to promote liberal
multiculturalism autonomously from other entities has been a
somewhat stable arrangement in the sense that the past
seventy years (let's periodize this from the 1968 SF State
strike) have seen thousands upon thousands of academic
workers and ex-academic workers see themselves as
"subversive" or pursuing equality, social justice, what have
you, against the powers that be; it's also true that this is
not a stable arrangement in the long run, in that any truly
subversive work (along with much that is not very
subversive) will be rooted out and what is left will have to
realign itself more closely with the interests of private
industry and the state -- as they are still what ultimately
determine the orientation of the academy and academic work,
in an overarching structural sense.

Every so often, a crisis emerges that, despite all of the
harm and destruction it might bring, can usefully expose
such contradictions and acts as a forcing function that
splits those two things apart. Academic workers who see
themselves as beholden to social justice but believe the
path to achieving that is to stick to the university's good
graces are then forced to choose. The choice goes like this:
You can either stick with the university’s premise, taking
up the idea that you are a small business owner
entrepreneur-ing within the ecosystem of grants and
employees, that you have rightfully earned or can earn a
respectable academic life, that you can become a homeowner,
get tenure, publish the essays and articles, go on the book
tours, and teach students as if that pattern is the way
things are and the way things will be; or you can reject it,
that nothing afforded to us is because we have deserved it
in any sense but because capitalism has structutral needs
fulfilled by the neoliberal university, that each of us are
precarious even if some of us seem to be well resourced at
some times, that this depends on the constant exploitation
of labor (of all of us, but of staff, grads, lecs, etc) and
violence towards others, and that nothing about this
fundamental arrangement will never change unless we at least
make a choice to each other, that we want something
different, that we are rejecting the individualism the
university imposes, that we reject the bourgeois ambitions
that our basic need for security and safety have become
entangled with and disguised as, and in turn that we are the
only guarantors of each others' safety and security (we are
each others' responsibility).

More concretely, that might mean actions like generously
distributing financial resources one has, constantly
establishing and disseminating an analysis of power and
governance and how it might be changed, working against any
mythology of the university and the ivory tower as either
uniquely good or uniquely evil, always trying to
conceptualize the world at large and our very small place
within it, and taking seriously the fact that no matter how
small our place is or irrelevant our position may be, it is
always our role to try to work towards the abolition of the
present state of things. I think that means an opening of
one's home, offices, library access, laboratory and
workshop, as we're able to; seeing all regulations and
boundaries as made-up and transgressable were it
strategically worth it for a socialist movement to do
so.^[This makes me remember instances in the past where I
was scolded for being too confrontational (it was over
stupid things; one memorable instance was in sending a very
polite email about a degree milestone to the program
director). "You've got to pick and choose your struggles." I
agree and I do; not every problem needs to be fought and
many stupid problems can't even really be seen as part of
the fight for socialism. We can also have strategic
disagreements over if any given struggle is "worth it" to
engage with, and still see ourselves as comrades in some
larger struggle for equality and emancipation. But I've
unfortunately never seen folks that give me such comments
ever again in such a space or seen a possibility to work
with them in or outside of the university to do something
more strategic, so I wonder (maybe ingenuously, because
there are obvious answers here) if these kinds of criticisms
were really about there being better paths to establishing
that other collectivity or socialist future -- if so,
wouldn't I have been able to meet them there?]

Anyhow, in part I am writing this kind of sappy reflection
because I am frustrated with the lack of class consciousness
and the way academic workers, both junior and senior, seem
to gobble up university propaganda, but it's also just a
reminder to myself and a signpost others can remind me of in
the future. I don't think I will ever be free from having to
make the choice between the interests of the university (in
which many of my own interests also lie) and emancipatory
ends (it isn't something I can state in a blog post and move
on with later); I too am an academic that has benefited from
many tens of thousands of dollars of others people's wealth
and time, been placed in this position because of society's
excesses and its needs (however small) for scholarship
around technology and capitalism. I'm still living within
these contradictions in which I adhere to a university that
ultimately serves the interests of capital (because, as I
noted above, I suppose I don't think any appendage of
capital is free from that implication). _These are the words
you once said, Nathan, where'd you go wrong? Where'd you go
wrong?_

Hopefully I am not so narrow-minded that I cannot take heed
of such comments in the future. And even if I am so stubborn
and hard-headed and have given up on any socialist or
communist horizon, the truth still remains: Everything that
is mine is yours, and it will be that way forever, long
after I have left the earth and long after any reference to
my name exists anywhere in the world.
